Era I

Prehistory

Before AD 43
I

Hillforts, farming and ancient landscapes

Long before the Roman conquest, communities were already shaping the land that became Northamptonshire. Worked flint, burial sites, settlement remains and later Iron Age earthworks reveal thousands of years of occupation.

People farmed the county's river valleys and higher ground, traded with neighbouring communities and created places for defence, gathering and ritual. Hunsbury Hill remains one of Northamptonshire's best-known links with its Iron Age past.

Era II

Roman

AD 43–410
II

Roads, villas and rural settlements

Roman rule brought Northamptonshire into a much wider network of roads, trade and administration. Routes crossed the county, while villas, farmsteads and settlements developed across productive agricultural land.

Archaeological finds show a landscape where local communities adopted Roman goods and customs while continuing to work the countryside. Evidence found around places such as Duston helps reveal everyday life in Roman Northamptonshire.

Era III

Anglo-Saxon

410–1066
III

Burhs, kingdoms and growing settlements

After Roman rule ended, Northamptonshire became part of a changing landscape of kingdoms, communities and new settlement. Place names, archaeology and later records preserve traces of this period across the county.

Northampton developed into an important fortified centre in the Midlands. Villages, estates and routes established during the Anglo-Saxon centuries helped create patterns of settlement that continued into the medieval period and can still be recognised today.

Era IV

Medieval

1066–1485
IV

Castles, abbeys, wool and royal Northampton

After the Norman Conquest, castles, monasteries and market towns became prominent features of Northamptonshire. Northampton Castle was an important royal stronghold, while religious houses such as Delapré Abbey shaped local landholding and community life.

Wool, farming and trade supported towns and villages across the county. Northampton also witnessed major national events, including the 1460 Battle of Northampton during the Wars of the Roses.

Era V

Tudor & Stuart

1485–1714
V

Civil War, religious change and the beginnings of the shoe trade

The Reformation changed churches, religious houses and patterns of belief across Northamptonshire. During the 17th century the county was drawn deeply into the English Civil War, with Northampton supporting Parliament and armies moving through the region.

The Battle of Naseby in 1645 became one of the decisive encounters of the war. At the same time, Northampton's community of shoemakers was growing, laying foundations for the industry that would later become one of the town's defining trades.

Era VI

Georgian & Victorian

1714–1901
VI

Industry, transport and the rise of boot and shoe manufacture

Northamptonshire changed rapidly as canals and railways improved transport and towns expanded. Traditional crafts increasingly moved towards larger-scale production, while new streets, factories, chapels, schools and public buildings reshaped urban life.

Northampton became especially famous for boots and shoes, supplying markets far beyond the county. The industry employed generations of local people and left a distinctive architectural and social legacy that remains visible today.

Era VII

20th Century to Today

1901–Present
VII

War, expansion, migration and a changing county

Two world wars affected communities across Northamptonshire through military service, industry and life on the home front. In the decades that followed, towns expanded, new housing was built and patterns of work and transport changed.

Places such as Corby experienced dramatic industrial growth and later economic change, while migration brought new communities and traditions. Northamptonshire today reflects this layered 20th- and 21st-century history.
