When Northampton had its own money

— ✦ —

Look up as you walk along the Drapery or across St Giles’ Square and you will see stone columns, carved pediments and grand doorways. Many of these were built by banks, and some of those banks once printed their own banknotes.

In Georgian times there was no single national banknote. Local merchants (often drapers, mercers or brewers who were already handling other people’s money) set up as bankers and issued their own paper notes. A Northampton shopkeeper might be paid in a note promising that a named local bank would “pay the bearer on demand”. Its value rested entirely on trust in the people who signed it.

Over the nineteenth century these small family banks merged into larger “joint-stock” companies with many shareholders. Then those were absorbed by the national banks whose names are still familiar today. Along the way, the right to issue local notes was given up, and the Bank of England’s notes became the money everyone used.

A short timeline

  • c.1781: John Jenkinson, a Towcester silk mercer and linen draper, starts a bank later known as the Towcester Old Bank.
  • 1783: Watkins & Co. opens as a private bank serving Daventry and Northampton.
  • 1815: After Jenkinson’s death, his bank is bought by John and Samuel Percival, bankers of Northampton.
  • 1836: Two big local banks are formed. The Percivals’ business becomes the Northamptonshire Union Bank, and Watkins & Co. becomes the Northamptonshire Central Banking Company.
  • 1838: The Central Banking Company is renamed the Northamptonshire Banking Company.
  • 1841: The Union Bank’s fine new building opens at 41 The Drapery.
  • 1885: The Union Bank gives up its right to issue banknotes.
  • 1888: The Stamford and Spalding Bank builds new premises in St Giles’ Square, today’s Old Bank.
  • 1890: The Northamptonshire Banking Company is taken over by Capital & Counties Bank. Its notes are still dated as late as that year.
  • 1911: The Stamford, Spalding & Boston Banking Company merges with Barclays.
  • 1918: Capital & Counties is taken over by Lloyds Bank.
  • 1920: The Northamptonshire Union Bank is acquired by National Provincial & Union Bank of England, a forerunner of today’s NatWest.

Northampton’s own banknotes

— ✦ —

Illustration only, in the style of a Victorian provincial note. It is not a copy of a real banknote.

Several Northampton banks issued their own notes, and examples survive in museum collections and turn up at auction.

  • Northampton Town & County Bank: a one-pound note dated 14 April 1825, issued for the partners Smith, Osborn, Hones, Smith & Osborn and signed by George Smith, has been sold at auction.
  • Northamptonshire Union Bank: printers’ proof notes for £5 and £20 survive. When the bank was formed in 1836, the Percivals’ Towcester bank was deliberately kept separate as a private bank so that the new company would not lose its right to issue notes. It finally gave that right up in 1885.
  • Northamptonshire Banking Company: the British Museum holds a run of its £5 notes dated between 1886 and 1890, issued right up to the year the bank was taken over.

The bank buildings you can still see

— ✦ —

Banks wanted their buildings to look solid and trustworthy, so they built in stone, with columns and carving. Several still stand in the town centre.

41 The Drapery: the Northamptonshire Union Bank

Built 1841 · Architect E. F. Law · Grade II listed

The grandest of the Drapery’s banks. It has a stone front with tall Composite columns rising through the upper floors and a pediment at the top. Look closely at the pediment and you will find a carved phoenix, which was the Union Bank’s badge. The building was later used by the National Provincial Bank. Title deeds for the site from about 1836 to 1860 are held at Northamptonshire Archives.

The Northamptonshire Banking Company

1836–1890 · Branches at Northampton, Daventry, Stamford, Wellingborough and Kettering

The Union Bank’s great local rival grew out of Watkins & Co., founded in 1783. It opened or took over branches across the county and kept issuing its own £5 notes until it became part of Capital & Counties Bank in 1890, and eventually Lloyds.

The Old Bank, St Giles’ Square

Built 1888 · Architect Charles Dorman

Built for the Stamford and Spalding Bank in a classical style, with Ionic pilasters, round-arched windows and two Palladian doorways. After the Stamford, Spalding & Boston Bank joined Barclays in 1911, it became a Barclays branch for many years. It later became a pub, first the Rat & Parrot and today the Old Bank, so you can now have a drink where people once paid in their takings.

Twentieth-century banks on the Drapery

The Drapery kept attracting banks. The former Westminster Bank at 8 Drapery and 1 Mercers Row (now Nationwide) was built in 1926 by Campbell Jones & Son with a classical front. At 48 Drapery, Glyn Mills Bank built modern premises in 1965–6, designed by John and Michael Chaplin.
